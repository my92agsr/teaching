The Triple Crisis

Three interconnected emergencies. One coherent response.

Public schools are the most democratic institution America has.

They take everyone. They don't turn kids away. They feed hungry children, support families in crisis, and hold communities together. When schools struggle, it's not because educators have failed. It's because we've systematically underfunded them, tied their resources to property taxes that guarantee inequity, and blamed teachers for outcomes driven by poverty.

The problem isn't public education. The problem is that we've never fully committed to it.

And now we face three interconnected crises that schools alone cannot solve, but that democracy cannot survive without schools addressing.

The Triple Crisis

We face three emergencies showing up in classrooms every day. They aren't separate problems. They're the same systemic failure manifesting in different ways. Addressing one without the others is rearranging deck chairs.

Crisis I: Literacy

Students cannot read. Not "reading below grade level." Cannot read. The science of reading has been clear for decades, but implementation has been sabotaged by ideology, underfunding, and the inertia of whole-language orthodoxy.

73% of Virginia fourth-graders score "proficient" on the state test. 31% score proficient on NAEP. That's not a measurement discrepancy. That's an honesty gap. We're lying to families about what their children can do.

What teachers see:
  • High schoolers who can't decode unfamiliar words
  • Content-area classes derailed by reading inability
  • Students who've learned to hide their illiteracy
  • Intervention programs that intervene too late

Without literacy, nothing else works. You can't think critically about a text you can't read. You can't evaluate sources you can't comprehend. The other two crises are downstream of this one.

Crisis II: Executive Function

Students cannot focus. Cannot start. Cannot sustain. Cannot finish. This isn't laziness or moral failure. It's the predictable result of developing brains marinated in attention-fragmenting technology and chronic stress.

Working memory under siege. Task initiation collapsed. The ability to hold a thought while developing another one... atrophied.

What teachers see:
  • Students who understand the material but can't produce evidence of it
  • Assignments started and abandoned, repeatedly
  • Inability to work without constant external scaffolding
  • The thousand-yard stare of overstimulated nervous systems

Phones are part of it. But so is poverty-induced executive function depletion. So is the anxiety of growing up in an uncertain world. So is the learned helplessness from years of being told what to do every six minutes by a bell.

Crisis III: Epistemic Capacity

Students cannot tell what's true. Don't know how to find out. Don't have the tools to evaluate competing claims or the disposition to try.

This isn't about "media literacy" as a unit in English class. It's about the fundamental infrastructure of democratic citizenship: the ability to encounter uncertainty, sit with it, investigate it, and reach warranted conclusions.

What teachers see:
  • Students who believe the first Google result without question
  • Inability to distinguish evidence from assertion
  • Intolerance of uncertainty that demands immediate, simple answers
  • Resistance to updating beliefs when confronted with contradicting evidence
  • Learned cynicism: "everything is biased, so nothing is knowable"

Intolerance of uncertainty is the deeper problem. Students (and adults) increasingly demand immediate resolution to complex questions. They reach for the first answer that reduces discomfort rather than the best answer available. They mistake confidence for competence and volume for validity.

Historical thinking, scientific reasoning, source evaluation... these all require the capacity to hold open questions, tolerate ambiguity, and reason through complexity. That capacity is atrophying.

The Crises Are Connected

You can't evaluate sources you can't read. You can't reason through complexity when your working memory is fragmented. You can't tolerate uncertainty when your nervous system is dysregulated.

The student who believes conspiracy theories often can't read the debunking article. The student who can't finish assignments often can't sustain the attention required for careful reasoning. The student who demands simple answers often learned that complexity is punishing and certainty is rewarded.

Any solution that addresses only one crisis will fail.

What We Believe

1Teaching is intellectual work.

Not delivery. Not facilitation. Not "implementing with fidelity."

Teaching requires constant judgment: reading a room, adjusting on the fly, knowing when to push and when to hold back. This judgment comes from relationship and experience, not from scripts or software.

2Funding is the foundation.

Most "achievement gaps" are funding gaps. Most "failing schools" are starved schools. Virginia's reliance on local property taxes guarantees that wealthy districts outspend poor ones, then we act surprised when outcomes differ.

You can't reform your way out of resource scarcity. Address the funding before debating the pedagogy.

3Technology must earn its place.

Screens aren't neutral tools. They reshape attention, interrupt presence, and substitute stimulation for depth. The burden of proof should be on technology to demonstrate it serves human flourishing, not on teachers to justify its absence.

A 1:1 Chromebook program with d = 0.16 effect size isn't innovation. It's expensive distraction with a press release.

4Uncertainty is a skill, not a problem.

The ability to say "I don't know yet" and keep investigating is fundamental to democratic citizenship. Schools that reward quick certainty over careful reasoning are training the wrong reflex.

Epistemic humility isn't weakness. It's the starting point for genuine understanding.

5Systems shape behavior.

We don't blame burned-out teachers, disengaged students, or frustrated parents. We ask: what system produced this outcome? What incentives are at play? What would have to change for different results to emerge naturally?

Individual heroism isn't a policy. Sustainable systems are.

6Progressive education is rigorous.

Student voice, project-based learning, formative assessment, restorative practices... these aren't soft alternatives to "real" education. They're demanding in ways that matter.

Rigor isn't suffering. Rigor is depth.

Our Approach

We name the crises clearly. We trace their roots honestly. We build policy that addresses causes, not symptoms.

We don't pretend that schools can solve problems created by inequality, algorithmic attention capture, and political polarization. But we insist that schools are where the response must begin, because schools are where we still have access to everyone.

We lead with values, arm with evidence. We use effect sizes strategically, not devotionally. When the evidence supports progressive practice, we say so. When it contradicts edu-fads, we say that too. When it's contested, we're honest about uncertainty.

We're Virginia-rooted. We're teacher-led. We're not going anywhere.

A Note on Evidence

You'll see us cite meta-analyses and effect sizes. We do this strategically, not devotionally. The problems with this approach are real:

  • Aggregation fallacy: Averaging across wildly different contexts flattens everything meaningful.
  • Measurement bias: Almost everything measured is standardized test performance. Creativity, collaboration, epistemic humility don't show up.
  • Status quo bias: High effect sizes often go to interventions that help kids succeed at the current game. But what if the game is broken?

We use the numbers because they're the language policymakers speak. But we never forget what they can't capture.

Where We Stand

What We're For

  • Public schools as democratic infrastructure
  • Equitable funding before reform mandates
  • Science of reading with adequate resources
  • Attention-protective learning environments
  • Epistemic humility as a teachable skill
  • Teacher professional judgment
  • Slower, deeper learning over coverage
  • Joy as a legitimate educational outcome

What We're Against

  • Ed-tech solutionism
  • Policy made by people who don't teach
  • Surveillance software as "engagement"
  • Teaching certainty where uncertainty is warranted
  • Proficiency theater that lies to families
  • Blaming teachers for systemic failures
  • Reform churn that burns out educators
  • Treating schools like factories
