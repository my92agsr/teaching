Introduction

What Teachers Already Know

Every teacher in America can describe the student who reads the words but cannot tell you what the paragraph means. The student who opens the Chromebook with apparent purpose and, fourteen seconds later, has lost the thread entirely. The student who encounters two contradictory claims and simply picks the one that arrived first, or the one with the better graphics, or the one a friend already shared.

These are not three separate problems. They are three expressions of the same underlying challenge — and the fact that we have treated them separately helps explain why so many well-intentioned reforms have underperformed.

This paper introduces the Three-Capacity Framework: a way of seeing the intersecting crises of literacy, executive function, and epistemic capacity not as isolated deficits but as a linked system.

It argues that American schooling is not merely struggling with achievement scores. It is struggling to reliably build the capacities that make achievement, judgment, and democratic life possible.

Third Period Labs exists to name that problem clearly, connect it to what the evidence actually shows, and build teacher-led policy responses that work at the level of real classrooms. We are not pundits. We are practitioners. And what we see from behind the lectern demands a more honest framework than the one we have been given.

Part I

The Three Capacities

Literacy: The Foundation That Isn't Holding

When we say literacy in policy circles, we often mean decoding — the mechanical act of turning symbols into sounds, sounds into words. That matters enormously in K-2 instruction, and the science of reading movement has been right to insist on systematic phonics. But decoding is the floor, not the ceiling.

The literacy that matters for civic life is deep reading comprehension joined to knowledge building: the ability to read a complex text, hold its argument in mind, connect it to what you already know, and evaluate its claims. This is reading as thinking, not reading as performance.

31%
4th graders at or above NAEP Proficient in reading (2024)
Stalled
Reading recovery since 2019, unlike math

It is worth pausing on that asymmetry. Math scores fell and have begun, in some states and some demographics, to climb back. Reading scores fell and have largely stayed down. The explanations are debated — disrupted early literacy instruction during school closures, reduced volume of independent reading, increased screen time displacing sustained text engagement — but the pattern is consistent enough to demand attention.

From the classroom: I can hand a student a primary source — say, an excerpt from Frederick Douglass or a government report from the Progressive Era — and watch them decode every word on the page. They can read it aloud fluently. But when I ask what Douglass is arguing, or what the report assumes about its audience, the comprehension collapses. The words were processed. The meaning was not built.

This is not a student failure. It is a capacity gap — and it is the gap that most of our accountability systems are not designed to detect.

Executive Function: The Capacity Nobody Measures

In the research literature, executive function refers to a set of cognitive processes housed primarily in the prefrontal cortex: inhibitory control (the ability to suppress impulses and distractions), working memory (the ability to hold and manipulate information in mind), and cognitive flexibility (the ability to shift between tasks or perspectives).

In the classroom, teachers use different words. They say: He can't get started. She starts but can't sustain. He finishes but can't go back and check. They describe students who lose the assignment prompt between reading it and opening the document. Students who are derailed by a single notification. Students who, when faced with a multi-step task, shut down — not from defiance but from genuine overwhelm.

The neuroscience is clear on at least one point: chronic stress impairs prefrontal cortex function. The same brain regions that manage executive function are exquisitely sensitive to cortisol, to unpredictability, to the kind of sustained low-grade threat that poverty, housing instability, family disruption, and yes, the post-pandemic social landscape impose on millions of students.

There is emerging evidence that even modest environmental interventions can help. Schools that have implemented meaningful phone restrictions — not performative policies but actual device-free instructional time — report measurable gains in student focus and task completion.

The unmeasured foundation: Despite growing recognition that executive function underlies virtually every academic outcome we care about, no national metric for it exists. We test reading. We test math. We test science. We do not test — or even systematically observe — the cognitive infrastructure that makes learning in all those domains possible.

Epistemic Capacity: The Question Democracy Requires

Can students tell what is true?

Not what they believe. Not what they prefer. Can they, when confronted with a claim, a source, a piece of evidence, deploy a reliable process for evaluating it?

The research suggests the answer is: mostly, no.

But here is where the diagnosis must be paired with instructional hope, because the evidence on intervention is genuinely encouraging.

Epistemic capacity is not a fixed trait. It is a teachable skill set. But it is a skill set almost no state standards require, almost no assessments measure, and almost no teacher preparation programs systematically develop.

Part II

The Interaction Effect: Why Single-Issue Reform Fails

The three capacities are not parallel tracks. They are load-bearing walls in the same structure, and the failure of one compromises the others.

Literacy → Epistemic Capacity

You cannot evaluate sources you cannot read. If a student lacks the deep comprehension to parse a complex argument — to identify its claims, distinguish its evidence from its assumptions, notice what it omits — then no amount of media literacy instruction will land. A student who cannot read a news article at the level of its intended audience cannot fact-check it, because they cannot understand what it is claiming in the first place.

Executive Function → Epistemic Capacity

You cannot reason when working memory is fragmented. Source evaluation is a multi-step cognitive task. You have to hold the original claim in mind, generate a search query, open a new tab, read a new source, compare it to the original, and synthesize a judgment. Every one of those steps taxes working memory. For a student whose executive function is already depleted — by stress, by distraction, by a device buzzing in their pocket — the cognitive chain breaks before the reasoning can complete.

Executive Function → Epistemic Maturity

You cannot tolerate uncertainty when you are dysregulated. One of the hardest things about real epistemic work is sitting with ambiguity. Sometimes the evidence is mixed. Sometimes the best answer is "I don't know yet." That tolerance for uncertainty requires emotional and cognitive regulation — which is to say, it requires executive function. A student in a state of stress-driven rigidity will reach for the first available certainty, not because they are intellectually lazy but because their nervous system is demanding resolution.

This interaction effect is the reason that single-issue reforms — phonics-only reading programs, standalone media literacy units, phone bans without instructional redesign — consistently underperform their promise. Each addresses one vertex of a triangle while leaving the other two unsupported. The framework holds only when all three capacities are built together.

From the classroom: I teach a unit on evaluating historical claims — propaganda analysis, source corroboration, the whole toolkit. The students who thrive are not necessarily the ones with the highest test scores. They are the ones who can read the source carefully (literacy), hold the question in mind long enough to investigate it (executive function), and tolerate the discomfort of not yet having an answer (epistemic maturity). When one of those is missing, the lesson still happens. The learning does not.

Part III

Structural Amplifiers

The Three-Capacity Framework describes what is weakening inside students' cognitive and epistemic development. But it does not operate in a vacuum. Three structural conditions amplify the crisis — not as separate emergencies but as accelerants on an existing fire.

Chronic Absenteeism

31%
Students chronically absent, 2021-22
~28%
Students chronically absent, 2022-23

Chronic absenteeism is not simply a logistics problem. It is a capacity problem. A student who misses 20 or more days of instruction in a year where foundational reading skills are being built does not merely fall behind on content. They miss the accumulated practice that builds fluency, the repeated exposure that builds vocabulary, the sustained engagement that builds the executive function habit of showing up and doing hard cognitive work.

The students who are absent most are disproportionately the students who can least afford the lost instructional time. This is an equity issue that masquerades as a behavioral one.

Teacher Capacity

Teachers are the delivery system for every educational intervention that exists. When the teaching force is destabilized — through turnover, chronic staffing shortages, and the relentless churn of new initiatives implemented without adequate support — the quality of instruction degrades regardless of how good the curriculum or policy looks on paper.

Implementation quality is the variable that policy most often ignores. A state can adopt an excellent science-of-reading curriculum. But if the teachers delivering it are in their first year, or are teaching outside their certification area, or received a single afternoon of professional development before being expected to execute it, the curriculum's evidence base becomes irrelevant.

Third Period Labs is founded on the conviction that teachers must be central to policy design, not merely its recipients. We know what breaks in implementation because we are the ones holding the pieces.

The Attention Economy

The device environment in which students live is not a neutral backdrop. It is an engineered system designed to capture and monetize attention — the very cognitive resource that executive function exists to manage.

The average American teenager's relationship with their phone is not analogous to previous generations' relationships with television or video games. The algorithmic feed is adaptive. It learns what captures attention and delivers more of it, in an endless scroll optimized for engagement, not comprehension. The notification layer interrupts sustained thought on a schedule measured in minutes.

Schools that have implemented genuine phone-free policies — where devices are physically collected or stored, not merely silenced — report improvements in student focus, peer interaction, and task completion. These are not transformative on their own, but they represent the removal of an active impediment. You cannot build sustained attention in an environment engineered to destroy it.

Part IV

The Standards-and-Signals Gap

One of the most persistent sources of confusion in education policy is the gap between what state accountability systems report and what national assessments reveal.

~73%
Virginia students meeting state reading benchmarks
31%
NAEP Proficient or above in the same population

The gap between 73% and 31% is large enough to generate justified public confusion and, too often, cynicism: someone must be lying. But the explanation is more important than the accusation.

NAEP Proficient is not the same as "grade level." NAEP's own governing board has stated this explicitly. NAEP Proficient represents "solid academic performance" and "competency over challenging subject matter" — a level that, by design, many students performing adequately for their grade may not reach. State standards, by contrast, are typically designed to identify students meeting grade-level expectations as defined by that state's curriculum. The two systems are measuring different things, at different levels of rigor, against different benchmarks.

State accountability systems are not capacity measures. A state test can tell you whether a student met a particular standard on a particular day. It cannot tell you whether that student has built the deep reading comprehension, the cognitive endurance, or the epistemic skill set that will serve them in contexts the test was not designed to simulate.

The danger of the standards-and-signals gap is that it allows policymakers and the public to believe the situation is either much better than it is (if they look only at state numbers) or much worse than it is (if they interpret NAEP Proficient as a basic competency line and panic accordingly). Neither reading is accurate. What we need are measures designed to assess the actual capacities we care about — and we do not yet have them.

Part V

The Measurement Spine

If the Three-Capacity Framework defines what matters, the Measurement Spine defines how we track it. Each capacity requires its own measurement logic — layered signals for literacy, proxy indicators for executive function, and task-based assessments for epistemic reasoning.

📖 Literacy Measurement
Layer 1
Independent national signal NAEP trend + distribution + below-basic shares. The only fully independent, longitudinal measure of reading capacity at scale.
Layer 2
Timely recovery signal NWEA/MAP interim trends for tracking whether stalled reading recovery is beginning to move. Provides the between-NAEP visibility that policymakers need.
Layer 3
State reporting signal Track state assessment results but never equate them with independent mastery. Useful for within-state trends; misleading as cross-state or capacity-level benchmarks.
🧠 Executive Function Measurement (Proxy Dashboard)
Chronic absenteeism rates — attendance as a proxy for the capacity to sustain engagement
Classroom disruption indicators — behavioral data as a signal of self-regulation capacity
Device policy implementation strength — measuring whether environments support or undermine attention
Student well-being indicators (CDC YRBS) — the stress and mental health context that constrains prefrontal function
🔍 Epistemic Capacity Measurement (Task-Based)
Civic online reasoning tasks — source checking and lateral reading performance on realistic evaluation scenarios
Conflict-of-interest detection tasks — can students identify when a source has a financial or institutional stake in its claims?
Civics knowledge baselines (NAEP Civics Grade 8) — the foundational knowledge layer that epistemic reasoning draws upon
Part VI

What Third Period Labs Will Do

Third Period Labs is a teacher-led policy initiative. We are not a lobbying organization, and we are not an academic research center, though we draw from both traditions. We are a space where practicing educators develop evidence-grounded policy frameworks and make them available to the people who make decisions.

Name the problem accurately

The three-capacity framework is our central diagnostic. We will continue to develop it, refine it against new evidence, and offer it as an alternative to the fragmented crisis narratives that dominate current discourse. Reading crisis. Mental health crisis. Misinformation crisis. These are real. They are also facets of a single structural challenge better addressed as a system.

Pair every diagnosis with instructional possibility

Systematic phonics instruction works for early readers. Explicit lateral reading instruction works for epistemic capacity. Phone-free environments work for executive function. Knowledge-building curricula work for deep comprehension. These are not speculative. They are demonstrated. The challenge is implementation at scale — a policy problem, not an evidence problem.

Center the teacher perspective in policy design

Third Period Labs operates from within the profession. Our founder teaches five sections a day. Our contributors are practitioners. When we say an intervention works, we mean it works under the actual conditions of American public schools — with thirty students, a seventy-five-minute block, and a borrowed laptop cart with four dead batteries.

Insist on honest measurement

We will advocate for assessment systems that measure what matters, not merely what is easy to test. This includes pushing for validated, scalable tools for assessing executive function and epistemic reasoning — capacities that currently go unmeasured at the national level.

Focus on Virginia, learn for the nation

Third Period Labs is rooted in Virginia — its schools, its policy landscape, its students. But the three-capacity crisis is national, and the structural amplifiers operate everywhere. What we learn here, we will share openly. What others learn elsewhere, we will integrate honestly.

A Closing Argument

There is a version of education reform that treats every problem as a content delivery failure. Students can't read? Better curriculum. Students can't focus? Better classroom management. Students can't tell what's true? A media literacy elective.

These are not wrong, exactly. But they are insufficient, because they treat symptoms without addressing the underlying capacity structure that makes learning — all learning — possible.

A student who reads deeply, who can sustain and direct their own attention, and who has reliable strategies for evaluating truth claims is a student prepared not only for academic success but for citizenship. They can read a ballot measure and understand it. They can encounter a persuasive argument and interrogate it. They can sit with complexity and not reach for the first simple answer that relieves the discomfort.

That student is what democratic education is supposed to produce. And right now, we are not reliably producing them.

This is not an indictment of teachers. It is not an indictment of students. And it is not an argument for despair.

It is an argument for clarity. For naming the problem as it actually is — a three-capacity crisis that demands integrated solutions. For building those solutions from the classroom up, not the statehouse down. For measuring what matters and telling the truth about what we find.

We start from the classroom. We build from the evidence. And we speak as teachers, because that is what we are.
