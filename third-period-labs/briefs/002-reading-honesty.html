The Reading Measurement Problem

What Virginia's Proficiency Numbers Actually Mean

Policy Brief #002 March 2026 Literacy Analysis
"When we tell the public that three-quarters of our students are proficient and the national assessment says fewer than one-third meet its standard, we are not lying — but we are not communicating clearly, either."

Executive Summary

1
Virginia reports that 73% of fourth graders are proficient in reading on the SOL assessment. NAEP puts that number at 31%. Both numbers are technically defensible. Neither number, standing alone, tells the public what it thinks it's hearing.
2
Post-pandemic reading recovery has stalled. Virginia's 2024 NAEP fourth-grade reading scores remain approximately 5 points below 2019 levels. Nationally, only one state — Louisiana — has surpassed its pre-pandemic fourth-grade reading benchmark. Grade 12 reading scores are 10 points lower than they were in 1992.
3
The science of reading movement has delivered real gains in early decoding instruction, but decoding is not reading. Comprehension depends on background knowledge, vocabulary depth, and sustained engagement with complex text — areas where policy attention and instructional infrastructure remain thin.
4
Virginia needs a literacy strategy that goes beyond mandates. Proficiency transparency, implementation support for teachers, early warning systems, adolescent literacy investment, and a decisive shift from worksheet-driven literacy to knowledge-building instruction would represent a serious start.

The policy direction: Virginia should build a literacy system that is as honest about where students are as it is ambitious about where they're going.

The Core Claim

Virginia has a reading measurement problem. It is not a problem of fraud or deliberate deception. It is a problem of two measurement systems using the same word — proficient — to mean fundamentally different things, and a public that reasonably hears that word as a common-language claim about whether children can read at grade level.

The Proficiency Gap

The explanation is not that one test is right and the other wrong. Virginia's SOL assessments measure mastery of Virginia's specific content standards. NAEP measures performance against a national framework with achievement levels set by a separate governing board. NAEP's own governing body has repeatedly noted that "NAEP Proficient" does not mean "grade level" — it represents a demanding standard that many education researchers consider aspirational rather than normative.

The policy problem: When a parent hears that 73% of Virginia fourth graders are proficient in reading, they hear a claim about competence. When the federal assessment says roughly three in ten meet its standard, the dissonance doesn't prompt nuance — it prompts confusion, distrust, or disengagement.

The result is policy miscalibration. If we believe three-quarters of our students are reading proficiently, we build policy for the margins. If the real picture is that perhaps half of our students read with genuine fluency and comprehension — a reasonable midpoint estimate — we need a fundamentally different level of investment and urgency.

What the Data Show

The Proficiency Gap Is Not New, But the Post-Pandemic Context Makes It Urgent

The gap between state proficiency rates and NAEP proficiency rates has existed for decades and is not unique to Virginia. What has changed is the post-pandemic trajectory. Virginia's 2024 NAEP fourth-grade reading score dropped approximately 5 points from 2019. Reading has been stubbornly resistant to recovery.

State Exceeding Pre-Pandemic (Louisiana)
  • Only one state — Louisiana — surpassed its pre-pandemic fourth-grade reading score on the 2024 NAEP. Louisiana's gains are attributed to years of coherent, standards-aligned curriculum reform that predated the pandemic.
  • Grade 12 NAEP reading scores are 10 points lower than they were in 1992. This is not a pandemic story. This is a generational decline in adolescent and young-adult reading capacity.
  • NWEA/MAP growth data suggest that reading recovery has been slower than math recovery nationally. Reading comprehension deficits compound over time and resist quick fixes because they are tied to cumulative knowledge and vocabulary exposure.

What Teachers See

Teachers across Virginia describe a reading landscape that the aggregate numbers obscure:

  • Students who can decode grade-level text fluently but cannot summarize what they read or make inferences across paragraphs.
  • Fourth and fifth graders who pass the SOL reading assessment but struggle with content-area textbooks in science and social studies.
  • A widening gap between students who read outside school and students whose only text exposure is classroom-assigned passages.
  • Middle school students who can answer literal comprehension questions but freeze when asked to synthesize information from two sources.
  • High school students who avoid assigned reading entirely, relying on summaries, AI tools, or class discussion to approximate understanding.

These observations are diagnostic signals. They suggest that Virginia's reading challenge is not primarily a decoding problem — it is a comprehension, knowledge, and engagement problem, and it intensifies as students move through the grades.

The Science of Reading: What It Gets Right and Where the Conversation Thins

The science of reading movement has been one of the most consequential education policy developments of the past decade. Its core contributions are real and important:

What the Science of Reading Gets Right

  • Systematic phonics instruction works. Explicit, systematic instruction in letter-sound relationships is essential for beginning readers.
  • The three-cueing system was wrong. Encouraging beginning readers to guess words from context rather than decoding them was a major instructional failure.
  • Early screening matters. Universal screening tools can identify students at risk for reading difficulty as early as kindergarten.

Virginia has taken meaningful steps with the Virginia Literacy Act (2022), requiring evidence-based literacy instruction, early screening, and intervention.

But the policy conversation has thinned at exactly the point where the instructional challenge thickens. Decoding is the foundation of reading, but it is not reading. The Simple View of Reading holds that reading comprehension is the product of decoding and language comprehension. Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient.

Language comprehension depends on:

  • Vocabulary breadth and depth. Students need to know many words and know them well — not just definitions but connotations, usage patterns, and relationships.
  • Background knowledge. What a reader already knows about a topic is one of the strongest predictors of how well they will understand a text about it.
  • Text structure awareness. Understanding how arguments are organized and how informational texts signal importance requires explicit instruction.
  • Inferential reasoning. Most meaning in text is implicit. Skilled readers continuously generate inferences connecting what the text says to what they know.

The Risk in the Current Policy Moment

States — including Virginia — will invest heavily in early decoding (correctly), declare the science of reading implemented, and neglect the knowledge-building and comprehension infrastructure that determines whether students who can decode will become students who can read.

The lesson from Mississippi is not just "teach phonics." It is: build a system — coaching, professional development, aligned materials, and sustained political commitment over more than a decade.

What the Strongest Counterarguments Get Right

"NAEP Proficient is too high a bar."

This is correct. NAEP's Proficient level is genuinely demanding, and the National Academies have formally recommended that NAEP reconsider its achievement-level labels. We use the comparison here not to claim that 69% of Virginia fourth graders can't read, but to demonstrate that the word "proficient" is doing different work in different contexts, and that the public has no easy way to know this.

"Virginia's SOL scores reflect genuine learning."

Also correct. SOL proficiency means something real. The question is whether those expectations, and the way we communicate about them, give parents and policymakers an accurate picture of student reading capacity — particularly the capacity to comprehend complex text across subjects.

"The science of reading has already addressed these issues."

Partly correct. The movement has correctly reoriented early reading instruction. But its policy influence has been concentrated on decoding and early grades. The comprehension side — vocabulary, knowledge, complex text, adolescent literacy — has received less policy attention, less funding, and less implementation support.

What Virginia Should Do

Five priorities for a literacy system that matches the scale of the challenge:

1
Proficiency Transparency Adopt a public reporting framework that contextualizes SOL proficiency rates alongside NAEP results, NWEA/MAP growth data, and other external benchmarks. Report cards should include clear language explaining what "proficient" means.
2
Implementation Infrastructure Invest in literacy coaching at scale, curriculum coherence with knowledge-building ELA curricula, and protected planning time. Mandates without infrastructure produce compliance without change.
3
Early Warning Systems That Trigger Action Link screening data to specific intervention protocols. Fund reading specialists and structured intervention programs. Ensure intensive support before the gap compounds.
4
Adolescent Literacy Revival Fund disciplinary literacy PD for middle and high school teachers. Invest in school libraries. Create incentives for knowledge-rich curricula in social studies and science.
5
Knowledge-Building Over Worksheet Literacy Shift from skills-based literacy to knowledge-building: content-rich curriculum, restored social studies and science instruction, vocabulary as a knowledge problem, and reading volume that builds comprehension.
A literacy system that is as honest about where students are as it is ambitious about where they're going.

Policy Recommendations

For Legislators

  • Require public reporting that contextualizes SOL proficiency alongside national benchmarks
  • Fund the Virginia Literacy Act with implementation infrastructure, not just mandates
  • Invest in adolescent literacy programs for grades 4–12

For School Boards

  • Adopt knowledge-building ELA curricula aligned with evidence-based literacy instruction
  • Request data on reading outcomes beyond SOL pass rates
  • Protect elementary social studies and science instructional time

For Administrators

  • Invest in sustained literacy coaching, not one-time trainings
  • Ensure screening data triggers intervention, not just reporting
  • Fund reading specialists and structured intervention capacity

For Teachers

  • Advocate for content-rich, sequenced curriculum over test-prep passage sets
  • Build vocabulary through sustained engagement with rich text, not weekly lists
  • Share what you see: the gap between decoding and comprehension is real

For Parents

  • Ask what "proficient" means on your child's reading assessment
  • Support reading volume at home — students who read more comprehend more
  • Advocate for school libraries and knowledge-rich instruction

Conclusion

Virginia's reading challenge is not a mystery, and it is not hopeless. The evidence base for effective literacy instruction is stronger than it has ever been. Several states have demonstrated that coherent, sustained reform can produce meaningful gains.

But gains require honesty about the starting point. When communication fails, so does the political will for the level of investment that real reading improvement demands.

Virginia's students deserve a literacy system that tells the truth about what "proficient" means — and builds the instructional infrastructure to make the word mean what parents think it means.
