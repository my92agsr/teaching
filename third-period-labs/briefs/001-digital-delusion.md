# Screens Should Earn Their Place in Virginia Classrooms
## Why Virginia Should Adopt a Purpose-First Standard for Classroom Technology

**Third Period Labs Policy Brief #001**

**Published:** March 2026
**Author:** Third Period Labs: Teacher-led Education Policy
**Series:** EdTech Analysis

---

### Executive Summary

Virginia should stop treating educational technology as presumptively beneficial.

The evidence does not support a blanket rejection of digital tools. But it does support a strong presumption against indiscriminate device use, especially when screens displace deep reading, handwriting, sustained discussion, and teacher-guided instruction.

The best available research points to three conclusions:

1. **Broad 1:1 device initiatives produce weak academic gains.** A major meta-analysis found an overall achievement effect around d = 0.16 for one-to-one laptop programs. Hattie's synthesis lands in the same place. That is not catastrophic harm, but it is also not evidence for a high-yield statewide strategy.

2. **Some core classroom activities suffer on screens.** Meta-analyses find consistent comprehension advantages for paper reading over screen reading, with larger penalties for expository text. Electronic note-taking is associated with lower learning outcomes compared to handwriting.

3. **The evidence is highly conditional.** Structured tools that provide guided practice, feedback, or adaptive tutoring show moderate positive effects (CAI at 0.47, ITS at 0.48). The question is not "technology or no technology" but "generic devices or evidence-based tools."

**The policy direction:** Virginia should adopt a purpose-first standard. Digital tools should be used for instruction only when they clearly support specific learning outcomes better than lower-tech alternatives, and only when schools can show attention safeguards, implementation evidence, and outcome measures.

---

### The Core Claim

Virginia does not need an anti-technology crusade. It needs a higher burden of proof.

For too long, educational technology has been treated as a modernization strategy instead of an instructional strategy. That is backwards. Schools should not begin with the question, "How do we get more devices into more classrooms?" They should begin with the question, "What learning problem are we trying to solve, and what evidence suggests this tool will solve it?"

That distinction matters because the umbrella term "edtech" hides enormous variation. The average effect of "technology" in meta-analysis combines very different interventions, from tightly designed tutoring systems to open-ended browsing environments.

**The policy problem in Virginia is not whether some digital tools work.** Many do. The problem is that schools have often treated device access itself as the intervention.

---

### What the Evidence Says About 1:1 Devices

The research on broad 1:1 laptop initiatives is much less impressive than the rhetoric that has surrounded them.

A major meta-analysis of one-to-one laptop programs found an overall achievement effect around d = 0.16. Hattie's synthesis lands in the same place, listing "one-on-one laptops" at 0.16.

That is not evidence of catastrophic harm. But it is also not evidence for a high-yield statewide strategy, especially once districts account for hardware costs, software subscriptions, management burdens, teacher training time, and the opportunity cost of not investing in more effective interventions.

**The sharper conclusion: general device provision is a weak intervention.**

By contrast, when technology is tightly linked to a specific learning function, results look better. Hattie reports 0.47 for computer-assisted instruction and 0.48 for intelligent tutoring systems. A meta-analysis of tablet-based mathematics interventions reported a pooled effect around d = 0.49, though with substantial heterogeneity.

That difference matters. It suggests the question is not "technology or no technology," but "generic devices or evidence-based tools."

---

### Screens, Reading, and Cognitive Friction

Any serious Virginia literacy agenda has to reckon with a now substantial literature on screen reading.

A major meta-analysis found a paper advantage over digital reading of roughly g = -0.21, and another systematic review reported g = -0.25 overall, with a larger penalty for expository texts (g = -0.32) than for narrative texts (g = -0.04).

That distinction is critical for schools. Expository reading is the language of civics, science, history, and technical subjects. If students are doing precisely the kind of reading that requires sustained attention, conceptual integration, and rereading, the evidence suggests print remains the safer default medium.

The case is even more cautionary with younger children. A meta-analysis of children's reading (ages 1-8) found that comprehension tends to be lower on screen, and adult mediation matters more than flashy digital features.

**This does not mean screens should disappear from literacy instruction. It does mean Virginia should stop assuming that digital reading is a neutral substitute for paper.**

---

### Handwriting, Note-Taking, and Memory

A similar pattern appears in the note-taking literature.

A meta-analysis of handwritten versus electronic note-taking found that "using electronic notetaking methods reduced measured outcomes," with an average effect reported as r = -0.142.

That effect is not enormous, but it is large enough to matter at scale. Handwritten note-taking often forces selection, summarization, and encoding, while typing can invite verbatim capture without equivalent processing.

**The policy implication:** Virginia should be wary of defaulting to typed note-taking in classrooms where note-taking is meant to deepen listening, summarization, and retention.

### Attention Is Not a Side Issue

The strongest policy argument against indiscriminate classroom technology use is not nostalgia. It is attention.

OECD reporting based on PISA 2022 found that digital distraction is widespread:

- Nearly **one in three students** across OECD countries said classmates are distracted by devices in most or every math lesson
- **59%** said their attention was diverted by other students using devices in at least some lessons
- Students distracted by peers' devices scored approximately **15 points lower** in mathematics

The OECD findings also point to a telling split. Moderate school use of digital devices for learning is associated with better performance, while leisure-oriented use is associated with worse performance:

- **1-5 hours per day of learning-focused use:** +20 points in math (OECD average)
- **More than 1 hour per day of leisure use at school:** -9 points in math
- **Students using computers 6+ hours daily:** 66 points lower than non-users

Experimental work supports the idea that multitasking is not a harmless side effect. A widely cited classroom study found that students who multitasked on laptops during lectures performed worse, and **nearby students were also harmed simply by being seated within view of those screens.**

This is where the current debate is often muddled. The issue is not whether a device can display educational content. Of course it can. The issue is whether an always-open device environment fragments attention so thoroughly that the instructional gains never fully materialize.

### What Pro-EdTech Research Gets Right

A credible brief should acknowledge what the pro-edtech case gets right.

The best argument for classroom technology is not that devices are inherently modern or motivating. It is that some digital tools perform valuable instructional functions well.

**Technology can help when it:**

- Delivers immediate feedback
- Supports guided practice
- Provides adaptive tutoring
- Expands accessibility for students with disabilities
- Gives students access to materials and communication tools they otherwise would not have

That is why Hattie's larger effects cluster around computer-assisted instruction and intelligent tutoring systems, not generic laptop access.

There is also a legitimate equity argument, though it is often overstated. A recent meta-analysis focused on less-advantaged students found that educational technology had a small, positive, statistically significant impact on achievement, with the strongest benefits coming from computer-assisted learning and behavioral interventions rather than simple access to devices.

**So the strong pro-edtech argument is real, but narrow:** technology can be worthwhile when it is targeted, constrained, and tied to a clear mechanism of learning.

**The weak pro-edtech argument** is the one schools too often default to: "students need devices because the future is digital." That claim is too vague to justify expensive, cognitively intrusive daily practice in classrooms.

### Virginia's Policy Contradiction

Virginia's own policy landscape makes this debate timely.

In July 2024, Governor Glenn Youngkin issued Executive Order 33, directing state agencies to produce guidance for "cell phone-free education" and requiring school divisions to adopt policies by January 1, 2025. The order explicitly contemplated strict limits during instructional time and referenced other personal electronic communication devices, while preserving school-authorized uses.

**That matters because Virginia has already accepted a core premise of the attention argument:** schools cannot treat distraction as a trivial side issue.

At the same time, Virginia continues to support educational technology infrastructure. The state's Educational Technology Notes program funds instructional technology, connectivity, and a continuing student-computer ratio target, and VDOE has continued statewide support for platforms through June 2026.

**Virginia has therefore created a policy contradiction.** On one hand, the state recognizes that unmanaged devices can impair focus. On the other, it still largely funds and governs school technology as if access itself were presumptively beneficial.

That contradiction is especially hard to defend given student performance.

On the 2024 NAEP, Virginia reported:
- **31%** proficient in grade 4 reading
- **40%** proficient in grade 4 math
- **29%** proficient in grade 8 reading
- **29%** proficient in grade 8 math

Analysts have pointed to a large "honesty gap" between Virginia's state-reported proficiency rates and NAEP benchmarks. Virginia's 2024 grade 4 reading proficiency was **73% on the state system versus 31% on NAEP**. Virginia's recent effort to raise cut scores is explicitly framed as a move toward more honest alignment with national expectations.

**If Virginia is serious about higher standards, then it should be especially cautious about instructional routines that may undermine reading comprehension, sustained attention, and core academic performance.**

### International Precedents

Virginia would not be acting in isolation.

**Sweden:** The government has explicitly moved toward more printed materials and less screen exposure, including public investment in textbooks and teacher guides, under a broader reading and attention agenda.

**UNESCO:** Digital technology, including smartphones, should be used in class only when it clearly supports learning outcomes. UNESCO reports that phone restrictions have spread rapidly worldwide, now covering 114 education systems (58% of countries).

**Netherlands (2024):** After national guidance encouraging phone restrictions, a government-commissioned evaluation reported:
- 75% of high schools saw better concentration
- Approximately two-thirds saw an improved social climate
- Approximately one-third reported better academic performance

Those are self-reports rather than causal achievement estimates, but they still show the perceived benefits of attention-protective policy.

**England:** Research exploiting policy variation found that school phone bans improved test performance for lower-achieving students, with estimated gains around 14% of a standard deviation.

These examples do not prove that every low-tech policy works. They do show that governments are increasingly treating classroom technology as a question of instructional quality and cognitive conditions, not just convenience.

### What Virginia Should Prioritize Instead

A purpose-first standard becomes more persuasive when paired with better alternatives.

Compared with generic device provision (d = 0.16), several interventions have stronger evidence:

| Intervention | Effect Size | Source | Notes |
|--------------|-------------|--------|-------|
| Direct instruction | d = 0.60 | Stockard et al. meta-regression | Well-defined instructional model |
| Teacher-student relationships | d = 0.53-0.72 | Roorda, Cornelius-White | Correlational, may overstate causal |
| Intelligent tutoring systems | d = 0.48 | Hattie synthesis | Structured, adaptive software |
| Computer-assisted instruction | d = 0.47 | Hattie synthesis | Guided practice with feedback |
| High-dosage tutoring | d = 0.37 | Nickow et al. field experiments | Randomized trials, high validity |
| Formative assessment | d = 0.19 | Meta-analysis | Reading achievement |
| 1:1 laptops | d = 0.16 | Hattie, Zheng et al. | General device provision |

Effect sizes from different meta-analyses are not perfectly comparable. They derive from different study designs, outcome measures, and populations. The numbers are useful as rough orientation, not as a precise ranking.

**The point is not that Virginia should blindly chase the biggest effect size.** It is that if the Commonwealth is deciding where to place scarce dollars and scarce instructional time, it should privilege interventions with stronger evidence and clearer mechanisms before defaulting to broad device-heavy models.

### The Purpose-First Framework

Virginia should adopt a simple rule:

**Digital tools should be used for instruction only when they clearly support specific learning outcomes better than lower-tech alternatives, and only when schools can show attention safeguards, implementation evidence, and outcome measures.**

In practice, that means every major technology adoption should answer five questions:

**1. Learning Goal**
What exact learning problem is this tool solving?

**2. Evidence Base**
What evidence shows this type of tool works in settings similar to ours?

**3. Displacement Cost**
What high-value classroom activities will it replace, and what are the tradeoffs?

**4. Attention Safeguards**
What safeguards will protect student attention and prevent leakage into leisure use?

**5. Measurement and Exit Ramps**
What outcomes will we measure, and what happens if those outcomes do not improve?

**That is not a ban. It is simply the policy equivalent of asking technology to show its work.**

---

### Policy Recommendations

**For Legislators:**
- Require VDOE to report edtech spending alongside student outcome data
- Condition technology grants on evidence-based implementation plans answering the five questions
- Fund an independent evaluation of Virginia's edtech return on investment

**For School Boards:**
- Adopt purpose-first technology policies at the division level
- Request data on device usage patterns and correlation with achievement
- Question default assumptions about technology in learning

**For Administrators:**
- Audit current edtech contracts and utilization
- Protect instructional time from technology-related distraction
- Prioritize professional development on high-impact instruction over device training

**For Teachers:**
- Advocate for paper-based options when research supports them
- Document instructional time lost to device management
- Share what is working (and what is not) with building leadership

**For Parents:**
- Ask how technology is being used in your child's classroom
- Request information on your school's edtech spending
- Support phone-free and device-limited learning environments

---

### Conclusion

Virginia should stop treating educational technology as presumptively beneficial.

The evidence does not support a blanket rejection of digital tools. But it does support a strong presumption against indiscriminate device use, especially when screens displace deep reading, handwriting, sustained discussion, and teacher-guided instruction.

A school system serious about literacy, attention, and honest standards should not ask whether a device is available. It should ask whether the device has earned its place.

**That is the purpose-first standard. And Virginia is ready for it.**
