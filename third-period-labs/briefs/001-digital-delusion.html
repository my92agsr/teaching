Screens Should Earn Their Place in Virginia Classrooms

Why Virginia Should Adopt a Purpose-First Standard for Classroom Technology

Policy Brief #001 March 2026 EdTech Analysis
"Schools should not begin with the question, 'How do we get more devices into more classrooms?' They should begin with the question, 'What learning problem are we trying to solve, and what evidence suggests this tool will solve it?'"

Executive Summary

Virginia should stop treating educational technology as presumptively beneficial.

The evidence does not support a blanket rejection of digital tools. But it does support a strong presumption against indiscriminate device use, especially when screens displace deep reading, handwriting, sustained discussion, and teacher-guided instruction.

1
Broad 1:1 device initiatives produce weak academic gains. A major meta-analysis found an overall achievement effect around d = 0.16 for one-to-one laptop programs. That is not catastrophic harm, but it is also not evidence for a high-yield strategy.
2
Some core classroom activities suffer on screens. Meta-analyses find consistent comprehension advantages for paper reading over screen reading, with larger penalties for expository text. Electronic note-taking is associated with lower outcomes.
3
The evidence is highly conditional. Structured tools that provide guided practice, feedback, or adaptive tutoring show moderate positive effects (CAI d = 0.47, ITS d = 0.48). The question is "generic devices or evidence-based tools."

The policy direction: Virginia should adopt a purpose-first standard. Digital tools should be used only when they clearly support specific learning outcomes better than lower-tech alternatives.

The Core Claim

Virginia does not need an anti-technology crusade. It needs a higher burden of proof.

For too long, educational technology has been treated as a modernization strategy instead of an instructional strategy. That is backwards. Schools should not begin with the question, "How do we get more devices into more classrooms?" They should begin with the question, "What learning problem are we trying to solve, and what evidence suggests this tool will solve it?"

The policy problem in Virginia is not whether some digital tools work. Many do. The problem is that schools have often treated device access itself as the intervention.

What the Evidence Says About 1:1 Devices

The research on broad 1:1 laptop initiatives is much less impressive than the rhetoric that has surrounded them.

A major meta-analysis found an overall achievement effect around d = 0.16. Hattie's synthesis lands in the same place.

That is not catastrophic harm. But it is also not evidence for a high-yield strategy, especially once districts account for hardware costs, software subscriptions, management burdens, teacher training time, and the opportunity cost of not investing in more effective interventions.

The sharper conclusion: General device provision is a weak intervention.

When technology is tightly linked to a specific learning function, results look better. The question is not "technology or no technology," but "generic devices or evidence-based tools."

Screens, Reading, and Cognitive Friction

Any serious Virginia literacy agenda has to reckon with a now substantial literature on screen reading.

Expository reading is the language of civics, science, history, and technical subjects. If students are doing precisely the kind of reading that requires sustained attention, conceptual integration, and rereading, the evidence suggests print remains the safer default medium.

This does not mean screens should disappear from literacy instruction. It does mean Virginia should stop assuming that digital reading is a neutral substitute for paper.

Handwriting, Note-Taking, and Memory

A meta-analysis found that "using electronic notetaking methods reduced measured outcomes," with an average effect reported as r = -0.142.

That effect is not enormous, but it is large enough to matter at scale. Handwritten note-taking forces selection, summarization, and encoding, while typing can invite verbatim capture without equivalent processing.

The policy implication: Virginia should be wary of defaulting to typed note-taking in classrooms where note-taking is meant to deepen listening and retention.

Attention Is Not a Side Issue

The strongest policy argument against indiscriminate classroom technology use is not nostalgia. It is attention.

  • Nearly one in three students said classmates are distracted by devices in most or every math lesson
  • 59% said their attention was diverted by others using devices in at least some lessons
  • Students distracted by peers' devices scored approximately 15 points lower in mathematics

The finding that should change the conversation: A widely cited study found that students who multitasked on laptops performed worse. But here's what matters more: nearby students were also harmed simply by being seated within view of those screens. Distraction isn't just personal. It's environmental. (Sana et al., 2013)

The issue is not whether a device can display educational content. Of course it can. The issue is whether an always-open device environment fragments attention so thoroughly that the instructional gains never fully materialize.

What Pro-EdTech Research Gets Right

Technology can help when it:

  • Delivers immediate feedback
  • Supports guided practice
  • Provides adaptive tutoring
  • Expands accessibility for students with disabilities
  • Gives students access to materials they otherwise would not have

That is why Hattie's larger effects cluster around computer-assisted instruction and intelligent tutoring systems, not generic laptop access.

There is also a legitimate equity argument, though it is often overstated. A meta-analysis focused on less-advantaged students found that the strongest benefits came from computer-assisted learning and behavioral interventions rather than simple access to devices.

The strong pro-edtech argument is real, but narrow: technology can be worthwhile when it is targeted, constrained, and tied to a clear mechanism of learning.

The weak pro-edtech argument is the one schools too often default to: "students need devices because the future is digital." That claim is too vague to justify expensive, cognitively intrusive daily practice in classrooms.

Virginia's Policy Contradiction

In July 2024, Governor Youngkin issued Executive Order 33, directing "cell phone-free education" and requiring school divisions to adopt policies by January 1, 2025.

That matters because Virginia has already accepted a core premise of the attention argument: schools cannot treat distraction as a trivial side issue.

Virginia Has Created a Policy Contradiction

On one hand, the state recognizes that unmanaged devices can impair focus. On the other, it still largely funds and governs school technology as if access itself were presumptively beneficial.

That contradiction is especially hard to defend given student performance:

Virginia's 2024 grade 4 reading proficiency was 73% on the state system versus 31% on NAEP. Virginia's recent effort to raise cut scores is explicitly framed as a move toward more honest alignment.

If Virginia is serious about higher standards, then it should be especially cautious about instructional routines that may undermine reading comprehension, sustained attention, and core academic performance.

International Precedents

🇸🇪

Sweden

Government has moved toward more printed materials and less screen exposure, including public investment in textbooks and teacher guides.

🇳🇱

Netherlands

75% of high schools saw better concentration after phone restrictions. ~2/3 saw improved social climate. (Self-reports, not causal estimates.)

🇬🇧

England

Phone bans improved test performance for lower-achieving students, with estimated gains around 14% of a standard deviation.

🌍

Global (UNESCO)

114 education systems (58%) now have phone bans. Position: technology only "when it clearly supports learning outcomes."

These examples do not prove that every low-tech policy works. They do show that governments are increasingly treating classroom technology as a question of instructional quality and cognitive conditions, not just convenience.

What Virginia Should Prioritize Instead

Compared with generic device provision (d = 0.16), several interventions have stronger evidence:

Intervention Effect Size Notes
Direct instruction d = 0.60 Well-defined instructional model
Teacher-student relationships d = 0.53-0.72 Correlational, may overstate causal
Intelligent tutoring systems d = 0.48 Structured, adaptive software
Computer-assisted instruction d = 0.47 Guided practice with feedback
High-dosage tutoring d = 0.37 Randomized trials, high validity
1:1 laptops d = 0.16 General device provision

Effect sizes from different meta-analyses are not perfectly comparable. The numbers are useful as rough orientation, not as a precise ranking.

The point is not that Virginia should blindly chase the biggest effect size. It is that if the Commonwealth is deciding where to place scarce dollars and scarce instructional time, it should privilege interventions with stronger evidence before defaulting to broad device-heavy models.

The Purpose-First Framework

Every major technology adoption should answer five questions:

1
Learning Goal What exact learning problem is this tool solving?
2
Evidence Base What evidence shows this type of tool works in settings similar to ours?
3
Displacement Cost What high-value classroom activities will it replace, and what are the tradeoffs?
4
Attention Safeguards What safeguards will protect student attention and prevent leakage into leisure use?
5
Measurement and Exit Ramps What outcomes will we measure, and what happens if those outcomes do not improve?
That is not a ban. It is simply the policy equivalent of asking technology to show its work.

Policy Recommendations

For Legislators

  • Require VDOE to report edtech spending alongside student outcome data
  • Condition technology grants on evidence-based implementation plans
  • Fund an independent evaluation of Virginia's edtech ROI

For School Boards

  • Adopt purpose-first technology policies at the division level
  • Request data on device usage patterns and achievement correlation
  • Question default assumptions about technology in learning

For Administrators

  • Audit current edtech contracts and utilization
  • Protect instructional time from technology-related distraction
  • Prioritize PD on high-impact instruction over device training

For Teachers

  • Advocate for paper-based options when research supports them
  • Document instructional time lost to device management
  • Share what is working (and what is not) with leadership

For Parents

  • Ask how technology is being used in your child's classroom
  • Request information on your school's edtech spending
  • Support phone-free and device-limited learning environments

Conclusion

Virginia should stop treating educational technology as presumptively beneficial.

The evidence does not support a blanket rejection of digital tools. But it does support a strong presumption against indiscriminate device use, especially when screens displace deep reading, handwriting, sustained discussion, and teacher-guided instruction.

A school system serious about literacy, attention, and honest standards should not ask whether a device is available. It should ask whether the device has earned its place.

That is the purpose-first standard. And Virginia is ready for it.
