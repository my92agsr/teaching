# Can Students Tell What's True? Building Epistemic Capacity in Virginia Schools

**Third Period Labs | Policy Brief 003**
**Spring 2026**

---

## Executive Summary

- **American students lack the tools to evaluate competing claims and, increasingly, the disposition to try.** Only 22% of eighth graders scored at or above proficient on the 2022 NAEP civics assessment. Stanford researchers found that students across grade levels consistently struggled to evaluate online sources, with 52% of students judging a misleading video as strong evidence.
- **The misinformation problem is not primarily a technology problem. It is a reasoning problem.** Research shows that analytic thinking improves the ability to distinguish true from false claims, but myside bias, familiarity effects, and motivated reasoning compromise even cognitively sophisticated individuals. Intolerance of uncertainty — the demand for immediate, simple answers — is a key driver of misinformation susceptibility.
- **This is teachable.** Civic online reasoning interventions have produced dramatic gains. In one study, students who could evaluate source credibility improved from 3 out of 87 to 67 out of 87 after targeted instruction. Lateral reading — the practice of leaving a site to verify its claims — can be taught in weeks and transfers across contexts.
- **Virginia should embed epistemic capacity — the ability to evaluate evidence, tolerate uncertainty, and reason about competing claims — across its curriculum.** This is not partisan media policing. It is process, verification, and reasoning instruction that belongs in every subject.

---

## The Core Claim

There is a capacity that underlies everything schools say they care about — critical thinking, informed citizenship, scientific literacy, media literacy — but that few schools teach directly and fewer assess. We call it epistemic capacity: the ability to evaluate how we know what we claim to know, to distinguish stronger evidence from weaker evidence, and to reason honestly when the answer is uncertain or uncomfortable.

Students do not have enough of it. And the gap is not primarily about intelligence, effort, or access to information. It is about tools and dispositions that can be taught but mostly aren't.

This matters for Virginia because every priority in the state's educational vision — from the Profile of a Virginia Graduate to STEM readiness to civic preparation — depends on students who can reason about evidence. A student who cannot evaluate a health claim online will not become a scientifically literate adult. A student who cannot assess the credibility of a political argument will not become a capable citizen. A student who has never been taught to sit with uncertainty will reach for the first confident-sounding answer available, regardless of its quality.

The good news: the evidence base for teaching these capacities is stronger than most educators realize, and the interventions are practical, not utopian. The challenge is that almost none of this is embedded in standards, assessed in any meaningful way, or treated as a shared responsibility across subjects.

## What the Data Show

### Students Cannot Do What We Assume They Can

The 2022 NAEP Civics Assessment found that only 22% of eighth graders scored at or above proficient. This was a decline from 2018 and part of a longer trend. Civic knowledge — the factual foundation for civic reasoning — is eroding, but the problem goes deeper than facts. Students lack the procedural knowledge to evaluate claims, weigh evidence, and reason about disagreement.

The Stanford History Education Group's research on civic online reasoning provides the most granular picture of the problem. In a study of 3,446 students across middle school, high school, and college, researchers presented tasks that real-world information evaluation routinely demands: assessing the credibility of a website, evaluating a social media post, determining whether a photograph was reliable evidence for a specific claim.

The results were consistent and alarming:

- **Students struggled across every task.** The challenge was not confined to younger or lower-performing students. High school and college students performed poorly as well.
- **52% of students judged a misleading video as strong evidence** for the claim it appeared to support, without questioning its source, context, or potential manipulation.
- **Students overwhelmingly evaluated websites by their surface features** — design quality, presence of links, professional appearance — rather than by investigating who was behind the site and what their motives might be.
- **Students rarely left a site to check its claims.** The practice of lateral reading — opening new tabs to verify information through independent sources — was almost entirely absent.

These findings are not evidence that students are foolish. They are evidence that students have never been systematically taught a set of practices that expert information evaluators use routinely. Professional fact-checkers, when given the same tasks, performed dramatically better — not because they knew more, but because they used a different process.

### What Teachers See

Teachers across Virginia describe patterns consistent with the research:

- Students who accept the first Google result as definitive and rarely scroll past it.
- History students who cannot distinguish a primary source from a secondary interpretation without explicit prompting.
- Science students who treat a single study reported in a news article as settled consensus.
- Students who, when presented with two conflicting claims, ask "Which one is the right answer?" rather than "What would I need to know to evaluate these?"
- A growing tendency to treat all contested claims as matters of opinion — "That's just what they think" — collapsing the distinction between informed judgment and uninformed assertion.
- Students who can identify "bias" as a concept but cannot explain how to account for it or what makes one biased source more useful than another.
- Increasing reliance on AI-generated summaries without any instinct to verify the output against original sources.

These are not failures of intelligence or effort. They are failures of instruction. Students default to the strategies they have — surface evaluation, authority deference, opinion relativism — because they have not been taught better ones.

## The Misinformation Research: Why This Is Harder Than It Looks

Understanding why misinformation is sticky — even for educated adults — is essential for designing instruction that actually works. The research paints a more complicated picture than "people need to think harder."

### What Helps

- **Analytic thinking.** People who engage in more deliberate, reflective reasoning (as opposed to fast, intuitive judgment) are generally better at distinguishing true claims from false ones. This is consistent with dual-process theories of cognition and suggests that teaching students to slow down and evaluate is a sound instinct.
- **Older age.** Adults, on average, discriminate between true and false news better than younger people, likely due to accumulated knowledge and experience. This is not reassuring for schools — it means students are at the most vulnerable point in the developmental trajectory.

### What Complicates

- **Myside bias.** People evaluate evidence more critically when it challenges their existing beliefs and more leniently when it confirms them. This is not a partisan phenomenon — it operates across the political spectrum and across domains. Teaching students to evaluate "the other side's" claims is easy; teaching them to evaluate their own side's claims with equal rigor is the real challenge.
- **Familiarity effects.** Repeated exposure to a claim increases its perceived truth, regardless of its actual accuracy. This is the "illusory truth effect," and it operates even when people have been warned about it. In an information environment built on algorithmic repetition, this is a structural disadvantage for accurate reasoning.
- **Motivated reasoning and motivated reflection.** Some research suggests that more cognitively sophisticated individuals are not simply better at finding the truth — they are better at constructing justifications for what they already believe. Analytic thinking can be deployed in the service of confirmation rather than correction. The relationship between cognitive ability and belief accuracy is real but not as straightforward as "smarter people believe truer things."
- **Intolerance of uncertainty.** This may be the most underappreciated factor. Individuals with low tolerance for ambiguity are more susceptible to misinformation because misinformation typically offers clear, simple, emotionally satisfying answers. The demand for certainty — the felt need for an answer *right now* — overrides the slower, less comfortable process of careful evaluation. Intolerance of uncertainty demands immediate simple answers, and misinformation reliably supplies them.

The pedagogical implication is significant: teaching students to identify false claims is necessary but insufficient. Students also need to develop the disposition to tolerate not knowing, to hold competing possibilities in mind, and to resist the pull of premature certainty. This is a psychological capacity, not just a cognitive skill, and it requires deliberate cultivation.

## The Hopeful Finding: This Is Teachable

Against this complex backdrop, the intervention research is genuinely encouraging.

### Lateral Reading Works

The Stanford History Education Group developed and tested a civic online reasoning curriculum centered on three core practices:

1. **Who is behind the information?** Investigate the source before engaging with the content.
2. **What is the evidence?** Identify what evidence is offered and evaluate its quality.
3. **What do other sources say?** Leave the original source and check its claims through independent, lateral verification.

The signature skill — lateral reading — involves leaving a website to verify its credibility and claims rather than evaluating the site on its own terms. This is what professional fact-checkers do. It is fast, learnable, and effective.

In a controlled study, students who received lateral reading instruction improved dramatically. On a key evaluation task, performance went from **3 out of 87 students** demonstrating competent source evaluation to **67 out of 87** after instruction. This is not a marginal gain. It is a transformation in capability achieved through focused, practical instruction.

### Gains Across Grade Levels

Civic online reasoning interventions have shown gains across middle school, high school, and college-level implementations. The skills are not developmentally restricted — younger students can learn simplified versions of the same verification practices that work for older students. The key is making the instruction explicit, practiced, and embedded in authentic tasks rather than delivered as a one-time lesson.

### Cross-Domain Transfer

Students who learn to evaluate sources in one context show evidence of transfer to other contexts, though transfer is strengthened when the same reasoning framework is reinforced across multiple subjects. This argues for a whole-school approach rather than confining epistemic capacity to a single course.

## What This Is Not

Clarity about what we are *not* proposing is essential, because epistemic capacity instruction is frequently mischaracterized.

**This is not media literacy as partisan gatekeeping.** We are not proposing that schools tell students which news sources to trust or which political positions are correct. We are proposing that students learn a *process* for evaluating any source — including sources they are inclined to agree with. The framework is procedural, not ideological.

**This is not "critical thinking" as a vague aspiration.** Many schools list critical thinking as a goal. Few define it operationally or teach it through specific, assessable practices. Epistemic capacity instruction replaces the aspiration with a set of concrete routines: source investigation, evidence evaluation, lateral verification, uncertainty acknowledgment.

**This is not about distrust.** Teaching students to verify claims is not teaching them to distrust everything. It is teaching them to calibrate trust — to distinguish contexts where trust is warranted from contexts where verification is needed. Functional adults do this constantly. Students need to learn how.

**This is not an additional subject.** Epistemic capacity instruction does not require a new course. It requires embedding reasoning routines in existing subjects — routines that enhance, rather than compete with, content instruction.

## What the Strongest Counterarguments Get Right

**"Students need knowledge before they can evaluate claims."**

This is substantially correct, and any epistemic capacity initiative that ignores it will fail. You cannot evaluate a claim about the American Revolution if you know nothing about the American Revolution. You cannot assess a health claim if you have no foundation in biology. Background knowledge is not opposed to critical reasoning — it is the raw material for it. The strongest version of this argument holds that the best defense against misinformation is a well-educated population with deep content knowledge. We agree. But we also note that content knowledge alone is insufficient. Highly educated adults fall for misinformation regularly, particularly when it aligns with their existing beliefs. Knowledge and process are both necessary.

**"Teachers already teach critical thinking."**

Many do, and they deserve recognition for it. Skilled teachers in every subject already push students to evaluate evidence, consider alternatives, and support claims with reasoning. What they generally do not have is a shared vocabulary, a common set of practices, assessment tools that measure reasoning quality, or institutional support for making this work explicit and systematic. The proposal here is to build on what effective teachers already do — not to replace it, but to scale it.

**"This could become politicized."**

This is a real risk, and it must be addressed directly rather than dismissed. Any instruction that involves evaluating contested claims can be perceived as ideologically motivated, particularly in the current political environment. The defense against politicization is procedural rigor: the same evaluation framework must be applied to all sources regardless of their political valence, and instruction must focus on *how* to evaluate rather than *what* to conclude. Teachers need training and institutional support to maintain this discipline. Schools that have implemented civic online reasoning curricula report that the procedural focus actually reduces political tension by giving students tools rather than conclusions.

**"You can't assess this meaningfully."**

Current standardized tests cannot assess epistemic capacity, but that does not mean assessment is impossible. Task-based assessments — in which students are given authentic evaluation tasks and scored on their process and reasoning — exist and have been validated in research contexts. The Stanford civic online reasoning assessments are one example. These assessments are more labor-intensive than multiple-choice tests, but they measure something that matters. Virginia could pilot task-based assessments without immediately attaching high stakes to them.

## What Virginia Should Do

### 1. Embed Civic Online Reasoning in Standards

Virginia's Standards of Learning should include explicit expectations for source evaluation, evidence reasoning, and lateral verification at every grade band. These expectations should be:

- **Concrete and assessable.** Not "students will think critically about sources" but "students will investigate the authority and motive behind a source before evaluating its claims."
- **Developmental.** Elementary students can learn simplified source evaluation ("Who made this? Why?"). Middle school students can learn lateral reading. High school students can evaluate competing expert claims and reason about evidence quality.
- **Cross-subject.** Epistemic capacity standards should appear in social studies, science, ELA, and health — not as an add-on but as a lens through which existing content standards are taught.

### 2. Require Task-Based Assessment

Virginia should develop or adopt task-based assessments of epistemic capacity. These assessments would present students with authentic evaluation scenarios — a website to investigate, a set of competing claims to adjudicate, a data set to interpret — and score their reasoning process, not just their conclusions.

These assessments should initially be low-stakes and used for diagnostic purposes: identifying which schools and divisions are successfully building these capacities and which need support. Over time, they could be integrated into accountability frameworks, but the first priority is measurement.

### 3. Create Cross-Subject Reasoning Routines

Epistemic capacity is not a single skill — it is a family of practices that take different forms in different subjects. Virginia should develop and disseminate subject-specific reasoning routines:

- **History: Sourcing.** Before reading a document, identify its author, date, purpose, and context. Ask: why was this created, and how does that shape what it says? This practice, drawn from historical thinking research, is well-validated and directly parallels lateral reading.
- **Science: Evidence evaluation.** Distinguish observation from inference. Evaluate sample sizes, control conditions, and replication. Understand that scientific consensus is provisional but not arbitrary. Ask: what would change my mind about this claim?
- **English Language Arts: Argument credibility.** Evaluate the logic, evidence, and rhetorical strategies of arguments in texts. Distinguish between claims supported by evidence and claims supported only by assertion or emotional appeal. Identify what an argument leaves out.
- **Health: Claim verification.** Evaluate health claims encountered online. Identify the difference between anecdote and evidence. Investigate the credentials and motives of health information sources. This is immediately practical — students encounter health misinformation daily.

These routines should be taught explicitly, practiced regularly, and reinforced across the school experience so that students encounter the same underlying framework — *Who says? Based on what? How do I check?* — in multiple contexts.

### 4. Teach Uncertainty Tolerance Explicitly

This is the recommendation most likely to be overlooked and potentially most important. Students need to learn that:

- **Not knowing is a normal and productive state.** Expert knowledge is characterized by an understanding of what remains uncertain, not by false confidence.
- **"I don't know yet" is a better answer than a premature guess.** This requires classroom cultures that reward intellectual honesty over speed.
- **Complex problems have multiple legitimate perspectives, but not all perspectives are equally supported by evidence.** The goal is not relativism ("everyone's opinion is equal") or dogmatism ("there is one right answer") but calibrated judgment: some positions are better supported than others, and we can reason about why.

Teaching uncertainty tolerance is partly a matter of explicit instruction — naming the concept, discussing why it matters, normalizing the discomfort of not knowing. But it is more fundamentally a matter of classroom culture. Teachers who model intellectual humility, who say "I'm not sure — let's find out," who treat student uncertainty as a sign of sophistication rather than weakness — these teachers build the dispositions that make epistemic capacity possible.

Virginia should include uncertainty tolerance in its framework for social-emotional learning and in its guidance for classroom instruction. It should be part of how teachers are trained and how instructional quality is evaluated.

### 5. Invest in Teacher Preparation and Support

None of this works without teachers who are confident and competent in epistemic capacity instruction. Virginia should:

- **Include civic online reasoning and evidence evaluation in pre-service teacher education.** Every teacher candidate, regardless of subject, should learn the lateral reading framework and practice applying it.
- **Fund professional development that is sustained, practice-based, and subject-specific.** One-day workshops on "media literacy" are insufficient. Teachers need ongoing support, model lessons, and opportunities to practice with feedback.
- **Create and curate open-source instructional materials.** The Stanford History Education Group's materials are freely available. Virginia should build on them with state-specific examples and tasks aligned to Virginia standards.

---

## Conclusion

The ability to evaluate evidence, verify claims, and reason under uncertainty is not a luxury competency. It is foundational to everything Virginia says it wants for its graduates: scientific literacy, civic readiness, career preparation, and the capacity for independent thought.

The research is clear that students do not currently have these capacities at adequate levels. It is equally clear that these capacities can be taught — practically, effectively, and at scale — through specific instructional routines that fit within existing subjects.

Virginia does not need a new program. It needs a new expectation: that every student, in every subject, is regularly asked to evaluate how we know what we claim to know — and is taught the tools to do it well. The cost of inaction is not measured in test scores. It is measured in a generation's capacity to navigate a world that will not stop demanding judgment just because schools never taught it.
