## Homepage (index.html)

### Hero Section

**Current:** "Three crises facing the _digital generation_."  
**Suggested:** "Three crises. One generation. Public schools are where we address them."  
**Why:** "Digital generation" is a label that might alienate readers before they scroll. The crises aren't only digital — literacy and epistemic capacity predate screens. Let the content make the tech argument.

**Current:** "Third Period Labs exists to name these challenges clearly and equip educators, legislators, school boards, and parents to meet the moment."  
**Suggested:** Cut entirely, or shorten to: "Naming the challenges. Equipping the response."  
**Why:** The CTA button already does the work. Listing four audiences in one sentence dilutes the punch.

### Crisis Cards

**Crisis I — Literacy:**  
"73% score 'proficient' on state tests. 31% on NAEP. That's not a measurement gap. That's a standards-and-signals gap."
→ **Updated per terminology alignment. Reframes as systemic measurement problem.**

**Crisis II — Executive Function:**  
"The thousand-yard stare of overstimulated nervous systems."  
→ Powerful image but doing double duty as symptom description and poetic closer. Consider saving it for the philosophy page where you have room.  
**Suggested card text:** "Working memory under siege. The ability to start, sustain, and finish... collapsing."

**Crisis III — Epistemic Capacity:**  
"Intolerance of uncertainty demands immediate, simple answers."  
→ Slightly abstract for a card.  
**Suggested:** "Students can't tell what's true. And they've stopped trying to find out."  
Let the philosophy page carry the deeper analysis.

### Principles Section

**Count mismatch:** Homepage says "Four principles, no exceptions" but the philosophy page lists 6. Either align the count or acknowledge that the homepage is a condensed version.

**Principle 2:** "Most 'achievement gaps' are funding gaps."  
→ **Extremely strong. Leave it.**

**Principle 3:** "The burden of proof should be on ed-tech to demonstrate benefit, not on teachers to justify its absence."  
→ **Your single best sentence on the entire site.** If you put this on a bumper sticker, you have a movement.

### About Section

**Current:** "Teacher-led means the people writing the briefs have spent years in classrooms."  
**Suggested:** "Teacher-led means the people writing these briefs teach. Present tense."  
**Why:** "Have spent years" sounds past tense. You're still in the classroom. That's your credibility.

## Philosophy Page (philosophy.html)

### Opening

"Public schools are the most democratic institution America has."  
→ **Great opener.**

"They take everyone. They don't turn kids away. They feed hungry children, support families in crisis, and hold communities together."  
→ **Strong rhythm. Leave it.**

**Current:** "...we've systematically underfunded them, tied their resources to property taxes that guarantee inequity, and blamed teachers for outcomes driven by poverty."  
**Suggested tweak:** "...and then blamed teachers for outcomes shaped by poverty."  
**Why:** The "then" adds sequential indictment — it happened in that order.

### Crisis I: Literacy

"Students cannot read. Not 'reading below grade level.' Cannot read."  
→ **Your strongest opening of any section. Devastating repetition. Don't change.**

"...implementation has been sabotaged by ideology, underfunding, and the inertia of whole-language orthodoxy."  
→ "Sabotaged" is accurate but strong. For a pure policy audience, consider "derailed." If you want the fight, keep "sabotaged."

"Students who've learned to hide their illiteracy"  
→ **Devastating. Keep.**

### Crisis II: Executive Function

"Students cannot focus. Cannot start. Cannot sustain. Cannot finish."  
→ **Perfect cadence.**

"...developing brains marinated in attention-fragmenting technology and chronic stress."  
→ "Marinated" is vivid but might read slightly flip for policy. Consider "saturated" or "immersed." Or keep it — it's memorable precisely because it's unexpected.

"Phones are part of it. But so is poverty-induced executive function depletion. So is the anxiety of growing up in an uncertain world. So is the learned helplessness from years of being told what to do every six minutes by a bell."  
→ **Brilliant. The bell line reframes the entire debate from "kids and their phones" to systemic critique. Don't change a word.**

### Crisis III: Epistemic Capacity

"Learned cynicism: 'everything is biased, so nothing is knowable'"  
→ **Names something most ed policy writing misses entirely. This is your intellectual contribution.**

"Intolerance of uncertainty is the deeper problem."  
→ This could be the thesis of Brief #003. Flag it.

### The Crises Are Connected

"Any solution that addresses only one crisis will fail."  
→ Strong closer. Consider making the interconnection more explicit:  
**Suggested:** "Any policy that addresses only one will fail on all three."

### Principles (expanded)

**Principle 1:** "Not delivery. Not facilitation. Not 'implementing with fidelity.'"  
→ Teachers will love this. Administrators might bristle. That's probably the point.

**Principle 3:** "A 1:1 Chromebook program with d = 0.16 effect size isn't innovation. It's expensive distraction with a press release."  
→ **Incredible line. Tweetable. Consider pulling this as a homepage quote.**

**Principle 4:** "Epistemic humility isn't weakness. It's the starting point for genuine understanding."

**Suggested:** "Epistemic humility isn't weakness. It's the only honest starting point."

**Principle 6:** Progressive education is rigorous. Make sure this section is as sharp as the others — it's doing critical work against the "soft pedagogy" dismissal.

## Brief #001: Screens Should Earn Their Place

### Title & Subtitle

**Title:** "Screens Should Earn Their Place in Virginia Classrooms"  
→ **Perfect. Implies a standard without being anti-tech.**

**Current subtitle:** "Why Virginia Should Adopt a Purpose-First Standard for Classroom Technology"  
**Suggested:** "A Purpose-First Standard for Classroom Technology"  
**Why:** Shorter, more assertive. The "why" is redundant with the title.

### Executive Summary

The three numbered points are clean. One issue:

"That is not catastrophic harm, but it is also not evidence for a high-yield strategy."  
→ This phrase appears **at least 3 times** across the brief. Good the first time. By the third, it reads like hedging. Say it once in the exec summary, then use "a weak intervention" or reference back in later sections.

### The Core Claim

"Schools should not begin with the question, 'How do we get more devices into more classrooms?' They should begin with the question, 'What learning problem are we trying to solve, and what evidence suggests this tool will solve it?'"  
→ **This is your framework in two sentences.** Consider pulling it up higher — maybe as an epigraph or homepage pull quote.

### Attention Section

"The strongest policy argument against indiscriminate classroom technology use is not nostalgia. It is attention."  
→ **Strong preemptive framing against dismissal.**

**Nearby-peers finding** (multitasking harms people who can SEE the screen) is your most surprising data point. Currently buried in a paragraph.  
**Suggestion:** Give this its own callout box or stat block. It's the single most persuasive data point for a skeptical reader.

### Pro-EdTech Section

"The strong pro-edtech argument is real, but narrow."  
→ **This line makes the entire brief credible.** You're not a Luddite. You're saying evidence supports specific uses, not blanket deployment.

"Students need devices because the future is digital. That claim is too vague to justify expensive, cognitively intrusive daily practice in classrooms."  
→ **Another tweetable line.**

### Virginia's Policy Contradiction

This framing is strategically smart — you're not opposing EO-33, you're extending the governor's own logic. That's how you get bipartisan traction.

## Big Picture / Cross-Page Suggestions

### 1. Voice Consistency
The homepage is punchy and declarative. The philosophy page is passionate and theoretical. The brief is measured and evidence-driven. Three different registers — mostly fine for different audiences. But the philosophy page occasionally drifts into academic prose that doesn't match the "teacher in the trenches" voice. Tighten those spots.

### 2. Pattern Repetition
You use "That's not X. That's Y." several times across pages. It's effective — but watch the frequency. **Twice per page max** or it becomes a tic.

### 3. Author Credibility
The site needs a clear "who is this person" somewhere visible. Your teaching philosophy paper (Freire, hooks, Vygotsky, Zwiers, translanguaging) is incredible — that's your intellectual DNA. But Third Period Labs doesn't reference any of it. A reader might not know this comes from someone with that depth.  
**Suggestion:** Add a short "About the Author" section on the brief, or link to the teaching philosophy from the TPL about section.

### 4. Quotable Lines — Summary
These are your strongest lines across all three pages. Use them in social, pull quotes, headers:

1. "The burden of proof should be on ed-tech to demonstrate benefit, not on teachers to justify its absence."
2. "73% score 'proficient' on state tests. 31% on NAEP. That's not a measurement gap. That's a standards-and-signals gap."
3. "A 1:1 Chromebook program with d = 0.16 effect size isn't innovation. It's expensive distraction with a press release."
4. "Students cannot read. Not 'reading below grade level.' Cannot read."
5. "The learned helplessness from years of being told what to do every six minutes by a bell."
6. "The strong pro-edtech argument is real, but narrow."
7. "Teacher-led means the people writing these briefs teach. Present tense."
8. "Learned cynicism: 'everything is biased, so nothing is knowable.'"

- Brief #002 (Literacy / The Standards-and-Signals Gap): Your research doc already has the evidence. Ready to draft.
- Brief #003 (Epistemic Capacity / Teaching Uncertainty): "Intolerance of uncertainty" is the thesis. Needs original framing but the philosophical groundwork is on the philosophy page.
