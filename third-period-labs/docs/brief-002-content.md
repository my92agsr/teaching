# The Reading Measurement Problem: What Virginia's Proficiency Numbers Actually Mean

**Third Period Labs | Policy Brief 002**
**Spring 2026**

---

## Executive Summary

- **Virginia reports that 73% of fourth graders are proficient in reading on the Standards of Learning (SOL) assessment. The National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) puts that number at 31%.** Both numbers are technically defensible. Neither number, standing alone, tells the public what it thinks it's hearing.
- **Post-pandemic reading recovery has stalled.** Virginia's 2024 NAEP fourth-grade reading scores remain approximately 5 points below 2019 levels. Nationally, only one state — Louisiana — has surpassed its pre-pandemic fourth-grade reading benchmark. Grade 12 reading scores are 10 points lower than they were in 1992.
- **The science of reading movement has delivered real gains in early decoding instruction, but decoding is not reading.** Comprehension depends on background knowledge, vocabulary depth, and sustained engagement with complex text — areas where policy attention and instructional infrastructure remain thin.
- **Virginia needs a literacy strategy that goes beyond mandates.** Proficiency transparency, implementation support for teachers, early warning systems, adolescent literacy investment, and a decisive shift from worksheet-driven literacy to knowledge-building instruction would represent a serious start.

---

## The Core Claim

Virginia has a reading measurement problem. It is not a problem of fraud or deliberate deception. It is a problem of two measurement systems using the same word — *proficient* — to mean fundamentally different things, and a public that reasonably hears that word as a common-language claim about whether children can read at grade level.

The Virginia Standards of Learning (SOL) assessments classify approximately 73% of fourth graders as proficient in reading. The National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP), administered to a representative sample of Virginia students in the same grade, classifies approximately 31% as proficient.

That is a 42-percentage-point gap. It demands explanation.

The explanation is not that one test is right and the other wrong. Virginia's SOL assessments are designed to measure mastery of Virginia's specific content standards. NAEP is designed to measure performance against a national framework with achievement levels set by a separate governing board. Crucially, NAEP's own governing body has repeatedly noted that "NAEP Proficient" does not mean "grade level" — it represents a demanding standard that many education researchers consider aspirational rather than normative. A 2020 report from the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine recommended that NAEP reconsider its achievement-level descriptors precisely because the label "proficient" invites misinterpretation.

So the SOL bar and the NAEP bar are set at different heights for different purposes. Fair enough.

But here is the policy problem: when a parent hears that 73% of Virginia fourth graders are proficient in reading, they hear a claim about competence. They hear that roughly three in four children can read adequately. When the federal assessment says roughly three in ten meet its standard, the dissonance doesn't prompt nuance — it prompts confusion, distrust, or disengagement.

The result is policy miscalibration. If we believe three-quarters of our students are reading proficiently, we build policy for the margins. If the real picture is that perhaps half of our students read with genuine fluency and comprehension — a reasonable midpoint estimate accounting for the limitations of both measures — we need a fundamentally different level of investment and urgency.

## What the Data Show

### The Proficiency Gap Is Not New, But the Post-Pandemic Context Makes It Urgent

The gap between state proficiency rates and NAEP proficiency rates has existed for decades and is not unique to Virginia. Virtually every state reports higher proficiency rates on its own assessments than NAEP does. This is partly by design: state assessments are curriculum-aligned and meant to be achievable benchmarks, while NAEP is designed as an external audit.

What has changed is the post-pandemic trajectory. Virginia's 2024 NAEP fourth-grade reading score dropped approximately 5 points from 2019. This is consistent with the national trend but inconsistent with recovery. Math scores, while also depressed, have shown more evidence of rebound in some states and districts. Reading has been stubbornly resistant.

The national picture is sobering:

- **Only one state — Louisiana — surpassed its pre-pandemic fourth-grade reading score on the 2024 NAEP.** Louisiana's gains are attributed to years of coherent, standards-aligned curriculum reform that predated the pandemic, not to post-pandemic intervention alone.
- **Grade 12 NAEP reading scores are 10 points lower than they were in 1992.** This is not a pandemic story. This is a generational decline in adolescent and young-adult reading capacity that predates COVID by decades.
- **NWEA/MAP growth data suggest that reading recovery has been slower than math recovery nationally.** Math intervention benefits from more clearly sequenced skill remediation. Reading comprehension deficits compound over time and resist quick fixes because they are tied to cumulative knowledge and vocabulary exposure.

### What Teachers See

Teachers across Virginia describe a reading landscape that the aggregate numbers obscure:

- Students who can decode grade-level text fluently but cannot summarize what they read or make inferences across paragraphs.
- Fourth and fifth graders who pass the SOL reading assessment but struggle with content-area textbooks in science and social studies.
- A widening gap between students who read outside school and students whose only text exposure is classroom-assigned passages.
- Middle school students who can answer literal comprehension questions but freeze when asked to synthesize information from two sources.
- High school students who avoid assigned reading entirely, relying on summaries, AI tools, or class discussion to approximate understanding.

These observations are not complaints about student effort. They are diagnostic signals. They suggest that Virginia's reading challenge is not primarily a decoding problem — it is a comprehension, knowledge, and engagement problem, and it intensifies as students move through the grades.

## The Science of Reading: What It Gets Right and Where the Conversation Thins

The science of reading movement has been one of the most consequential education policy developments of the past decade. Its core contributions are real and important:

- **Systematic phonics instruction works.** Decades of converging evidence from cognitive science, reading research, and classroom trials demonstrate that explicit, systematic instruction in letter-sound relationships is essential for beginning readers and superior to implicit or incidental phonics approaches.
- **The three-cueing system was wrong.** The widespread practice of encouraging beginning readers to guess words from context, picture cues, or sentence structure — rather than decoding them — was inconsistent with how skilled reading develops. The science of reading movement correctly identified this as a major instructional failure.
- **Early screening matters.** Universal screening tools like DIBELS and AIMSweb can identify students at risk for reading difficulty as early as kindergarten, enabling timely intervention.

Virginia has taken meaningful steps in this direction. The Virginia Literacy Act (2022) requires evidence-based literacy instruction, early screening, and intervention. These are positive developments.

But the policy conversation has thinned at exactly the point where the instructional challenge thickens. Decoding is the foundation of reading, but it is not reading. The Simple View of Reading — the foundational framework for much science-of-reading policy — holds that reading comprehension is the product of decoding and language comprehension. Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient.

Language comprehension — the ability to understand the meaning of connected text once the words are decoded — depends on:

- **Vocabulary breadth and depth.** Students need to know many words and know them well — not just definitions but connotations, usage patterns, and relationships to other words.
- **Background knowledge.** Comprehension research consistently shows that what a reader already knows about a topic is one of the strongest predictors of how well they will understand a text about that topic. This is not a skill that can be taught in isolation; it is built through sustained exposure to rich content.
- **Text structure awareness.** Understanding how arguments are organized, how narratives develop, and how informational texts signal importance requires explicit instruction and extensive practice with complex, authentic texts.
- **Inferential reasoning.** Most meaning in text is implicit. Skilled readers continuously generate inferences, connecting what the text says to what they know. This capacity develops through practice with texts that demand it.

The risk in the current policy moment is that states — including Virginia — will invest heavily in early decoding (correctly), declare the science of reading implemented, and neglect the knowledge-building and comprehension infrastructure that determines whether students who can decode will become students who can read.

Mississippi's literacy reforms are frequently — and rightly — cited as evidence that coherent implementation works. Mississippi's NAEP fourth-grade reading scores rose significantly after the state implemented the Literacy-Based Promotion Act (2013), which included phonics-based instruction, intensive intervention, and a third-grade retention policy. But Mississippi's approach was notable not just for its phonics mandate but for its implementation infrastructure: coaching, professional development, aligned materials, and sustained political commitment over more than a decade.

The lesson from Mississippi is not just "teach phonics." It is: build a system.

## What the Strongest Counterarguments Get Right

**"NAEP Proficient is too high a bar, and comparing it to SOL proficiency is misleading."**

This is correct. NAEP's Proficient level is genuinely demanding, and the National Academies have formally recommended that NAEP reconsider its achievement-level labels. NAEP Basic — which captures a larger share of students — may be closer to what most people mean by "grade level." Using the gap between SOL Proficient and NAEP Proficient as a shock statistic without context is analytically careless.

We use the comparison here not to claim that 69% of Virginia fourth graders can't read, but to demonstrate that the word "proficient" is doing different work in different contexts, and that the public has no easy way to know this. The measurement problem is real even if the specific numbers require careful interpretation.

**"Virginia's SOL scores reflect genuine learning aligned to Virginia's standards."**

Also correct. SOL proficiency means something real. A student who passes the SOL reading assessment has demonstrated competence against a defined set of expectations. The question is whether those expectations, and the way we communicate about them, give parents and policymakers an accurate picture of student reading capacity — particularly the capacity to comprehend complex text across subjects.

**"The science of reading has already addressed these issues."**

Partly correct. The science of reading movement has correctly reoriented early reading instruction and deserves credit for that. But the movement's policy influence has been concentrated on decoding and early grades. The comprehension side of the equation — vocabulary, knowledge, complex text, adolescent literacy — has received less policy attention, less funding, and less implementation support. Acknowledging what the movement has accomplished is not the same as pretending the job is done.

## What Virginia Should Do

### 1. Proficiency Transparency

Virginia should adopt a public reporting framework that contextualizes SOL proficiency rates alongside NAEP results, NWEA/MAP growth data, and other external benchmarks. This is not about replacing the SOL — it is about ensuring that families and policymakers can interpret SOL results in context. Report cards and school profiles should include clear language explaining what "proficient" means on the SOL and how it relates to national benchmarks. The goal is not to alarm — it is to inform.

### 2. Implementation Infrastructure, Not Just Mandates

The Virginia Literacy Act sets the right direction. But mandates without infrastructure produce compliance without change. Virginia should invest in:

- **Literacy coaching at scale.** Not one-time trainings but sustained, job-embedded coaching that helps teachers translate evidence-based principles into daily instructional practice.
- **Curriculum coherence.** Encouraging or requiring the adoption of high-quality, knowledge-building ELA curricula that integrate content-rich texts across grade levels. Louisiana's curriculum transparency initiative offers a model.
- **Protected planning time.** Teachers cannot implement complex literacy instruction without time to plan, collaborate, and study student work. This requires schedule-level commitments from divisions and schools.

### 3. Early Warning Systems That Trigger Action

Universal screening is valuable only if it triggers timely, effective intervention. Virginia should ensure that:

- Screening data are linked to specific intervention protocols, not just reported.
- Intervention capacity exists at every school — not as a mandate to be absorbed by existing staff, but as a funded commitment to reading specialists, tutors, and structured intervention programs.
- Students who are not reading on track by the end of first grade receive intensive support before the gap compounds.

### 4. Adolescent Literacy Revival

Virginia's literacy investment must extend beyond the early grades. The Grade 12 NAEP decline is not a decoding problem — it is a comprehension, engagement, and complexity problem. Virginia should:

- Fund disciplinary literacy professional development for middle and high school teachers across all content areas.
- Invest in school library collections and staffed library programs.
- Create incentives for divisions to adopt knowledge-rich curricula in social studies and science that build the background knowledge on which reading comprehension depends.

### 5. Knowledge-Building Over Worksheet Literacy

The single most consequential shift Virginia can make is from a skills-based literacy model — where reading instruction consists primarily of practicing discrete comprehension strategies on disconnected passages — to a knowledge-building model where students read widely, deeply, and across content domains.

This means:

- Prioritizing content-rich, sequenced curriculum over test-prep passage sets.
- Restoring meaningful social studies and science instruction in elementary grades, where these subjects have been crowded out by reading and math intervention blocks.
- Treating vocabulary as a knowledge problem, not a memorization problem — building word knowledge through sustained engagement with rich text, not through weekly vocabulary lists.
- Recognizing that reading volume matters. Students who read more know more, and students who know more comprehend more. Policy should support access to books, time to read, and classroom cultures that value reading.

---

## Conclusion

Virginia's reading challenge is not a mystery, and it is not hopeless. The evidence base for effective literacy instruction is stronger than it has ever been. Several states have demonstrated that coherent, sustained reform can produce meaningful gains.

But gains require honesty about the starting point. When we tell the public that three-quarters of our students are proficient and the national assessment says fewer than one-third meet its standard, we are not lying — but we are not communicating clearly, either. And when communication fails, so does the political will for the level of investment that real reading improvement demands.

Virginia's students deserve a literacy system that is as honest about where they are as it is ambitious about where they're going. That begins with telling the truth about what "proficient" means — and building the instructional infrastructure to make the word mean what parents think it means.
