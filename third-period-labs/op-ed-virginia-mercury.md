# Virginia Banned Phones. Why Are We Still Handing Out Chromebooks?

*Op-Ed for Virginia Mercury*
*By Matt [Last Name], Founder, Third Period Labs*

---

Last summer, Governor Youngkin signed Executive Order 33, requiring Virginia schools to go phone-free from bell to bell. The policy took effect January 1st. As a teacher, I applauded it. I've watched students struggle to maintain focus while notifications pulse in their pockets. The research is clear: phones in classrooms hurt learning.

But here's what I can't stop thinking about: why did we ban the phones and keep the Chromebooks?

Every day, Virginia students sit in front of school-issued laptops for hours. We call it "learning." But a growing body of research suggests we should call it something else: a very expensive distraction.

Dr. Jared Cooney Horvath, a neuroscientist who has worked at Harvard and the University of Melbourne, recently synthesized 398 meta-analyses covering over 21,000 studies on educational technology. His findings are damning. One-to-one laptop programs - the kind Virginia has spent millions deploying - produce an effect size of just +0.16. In research terms, that's essentially nothing.

It gets worse. When students read on screens instead of paper, comprehension drops. For informational text - the kind we use in social studies, science, and every content area that matters - the effect size is -0.29. Students literally understand less when we put the material on a screen.

Meanwhile, the 2022 PISA assessment found that students who use computers six or more hours daily score 66 points lower than non-users. That's equivalent to dropping from the 50th percentile to the 24th. The OECD's conclusion was blunt: "Technology used for leisure rather than instruction often seems to be associated with poorer results."

Virginia's students are struggling. The 2024 NAEP found that only 31% of our fourth graders met its "Proficient" benchmark — a demanding standard that NAEP's own governing board says does not equate to grade-level reading. Only 29% of eighth graders are proficient in reading or math. We rank among the worst in the nation for the gap between our state test scores and national benchmarks — what researchers call the "standards-and-signals gap." We tell parents their kids are proficient when national assessments paint a very different picture.

We have spent billions on devices. We have not spent billions on results.

I'm not arguing we throw every laptop in the dumpster. Technology works when it's targeted: intelligent tutoring systems for struggling readers, assistive technology for students with disabilities, specific applications with specific purposes. The research supports these uses.

What the research doesn't support is the default assumption that more screens mean more learning. We've been sold a story by edtech vendors, and we've bought it with public money.

Governor Youngkin got the phone policy right. The logic was simple: devices that distract students should be removed from classrooms. But that logic doesn't stop at the devices students own. It extends to the devices we hand them.

Virginia should adopt what I call a "purpose-first" framework for educational technology. Before a school deploys devices or software, it should identify the specific learning outcome, cite supporting research, and establish measurable success criteria. No technology without a target.

We should also audit what we're already spending. How much does Virginia invest in edtech annually? What outcomes can we point to? If a program can't demonstrate results, it should be sunsetted, and the money redirected to what actually works: smaller class sizes, literacy coaching, high-dosage tutoring, and professional development focused on instruction rather than device management.

The phone ban was a start. But if we're serious about reclaiming our classrooms, we need to ask harder questions about the screens we're still putting in front of our kids.

The research is there. The question is whether we're ready to read it.
