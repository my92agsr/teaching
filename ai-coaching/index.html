AI Coaching Framework

AI That Serves Kids, Not Replaces Teachers

An equity-centered approach to AI in instruction — grounded in language development, teacher judgment, and what actually happens in classrooms.

The Starting Point

Teaching is intellectual work. It requires constant judgment: reading a room, adjusting on the fly, knowing when to push and when to hold back. That judgment comes from relationship and experience, not from scripts or software.

AI does not replace that judgment. It never will. But used well — with intention, with guardrails, with equity at the center — it can extend what a skilled teacher is already doing. It can lower barriers for the kids who need the most support. It can free up the cognitive bandwidth that teachers spend on tasks that don't require human expertise, so they can spend it where it matters: on kids.

That's the framework. Not hype. Not fear. Disciplined, equity-centered use of tools that earn their place in classrooms.

Three Pillars

⚙
AI as Scaffold, Not Crutch
AI should reduce barriers to entry, not remove the cognitive work. A translation scaffold that helps an ELL access a primary source is different from a tool that summarizes the source for them. The first builds capacity. The second replaces it.
✍
Language Development First
Informed by Zwiers' academic language framework: AI tools should amplify opportunities for students to use and produce complex language, not bypass it. Sentence frames, vocabulary previews, and L1 bridges — generated and adapted in real time.
⚗
Teacher Agency Preserved
The teacher decides what to use, when, and why. AI coaches help teachers evaluate tools against their own professional judgment, not hand them a mandate from above. Implementation fidelity means nothing if teachers don't understand the why.

What I Believe About AI in Schools

1

Technology must earn its place.

Screens aren't neutral tools. They reshape attention, interrupt presence, and substitute stimulation for depth. The burden of proof should be on AI to demonstrate it serves human flourishing — not on teachers to justify why they don't use it.

2

Equity is the first filter.

Before asking "does this tool work?", ask "who does this tool work for?" AI that accelerates proficient students while leaving ELLs, students with IEPs, and under-resourced classrooms behind is not innovation. It's a new form of the same old gap.

3

Academic language is the lever.

Zwiers' research is clear: academic language development doesn't happen through exposure alone. It requires structured interaction, purposeful output, and scaffolded complexity. AI can generate those scaffolds faster than any teacher working alone — but only if the teacher knows what good scaffolding looks like.

4

Coaching is teaching.

Adult learners need the same things students need: clear models, safe practice, timely feedback, and respect for what they already know. A PD session that hands teachers a login and says "go explore" is as ineffective as a lesson with no objectives. AI coaching must be structured, responsive, and grounded in practice.

5

Build capacity, not dependency.

The goal of AI coaching is to make teachers more capable, not more reliant. Every tool introduction should come with an exit ramp: what does this look like without the tool? If the answer is "it doesn't work," the tool hasn't built capacity. It has created a dependency.

6

Uncertainty is a skill, not a problem.

AI is changing fast. Anyone who claims to have it all figured out is selling something. The honest posture is disciplined experimentation: try it, measure it, adjust. Teach teachers to evaluate AI the way we teach students to evaluate sources — with epistemic humility and a process for inquiry.

What This Looks Like in Practice

ESOL / Multilingual Learners
L1 Bridge Scaffolds
AI-generated vocabulary previews in a student's home language, paired with Zwiers-style sentence frames that scaffold academic output in English. The student accesses the content through their strongest language and produces evidence in the target language.
Social Studies
Source Complexity Laddering
AI adapts primary source reading levels while preserving the original argument structure. Students at different proficiency levels engage with the same historical question, building toward the same analytical skill.
Teacher Workflow
Formative Assessment Generation
Teachers describe their learning target; AI generates aligned formative checks at multiple DOK levels. The teacher reviews, selects, and modifies. The judgment stays with the teacher. The production time drops from 45 minutes to 10.
Professional Development
Practice-Embedded Coaching
Instead of one-shot PD, AI coaching embeds in teacher workflows: co-planning a lesson with AI scaffolds, observing implementation, reflecting on what worked. The cycle repeats. The learning compounds.

Where I Stand

What I'm For

  • AI that amplifies teacher expertise
  • Equity as the first design filter
  • Structured, Zwiers-informed language scaffolds
  • Practice-embedded professional development
  • Disciplined experimentation over mandates
  • Building capacity in teachers, not dependency on tools
  • Honest acknowledgment of what we don't yet know

What I'm Against

  • AI solutionism and vendor hype
  • Tools adopted without an equity analysis
  • PD that starts and ends with a product demo
  • Replacing teacher judgment with algorithmic recommendation
  • Surveillance features marketed as "engagement"
  • "AI-proof" assignments that punish instead of adapt
  • Innovation theater that burns out teachers

The Connection to Capacity

This framework doesn't exist in a vacuum. It connects directly to the three-capacity crisis I've been writing about through Third Period Labs: literacy, executive function, and epistemic capacity.

AI coaching done right addresses all three. It builds literacy through language-rich scaffolds. It protects executive function by reducing cognitive overload on teachers, freeing them to teach deeper. And it strengthens epistemic capacity by teaching both teachers and students to evaluate AI outputs critically — to ask not just "what did the tool produce?" but "is this accurate, complete, and useful?"

AI that doesn't connect to these capacities is noise. AI that does is infrastructure.

Who I Am

I'm a middle school Social Studies and ESOL teacher in Prince William County Public Schools. Eight-plus years in K-12. Master's Certificate in TESOL from George Mason University. Google Certified Educator, Levels 1 and 2. I've coached adults through GMU's TESOL program and built AI tools and systems outside of school because I believe educators should be shaping these tools, not waiting for vendors to shape them for us.

I teach five sections a day. When I say an AI tool works, I mean it works with thirty students, a seventy-five-minute block, and a borrowed laptop cart. That's the standard. That's the only standard that matters.
